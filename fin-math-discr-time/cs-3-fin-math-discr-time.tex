Split \( [0, T] \) into \( N \) parts, assume that
\( \lim_{N \to \infty} (1 + r_N)^N = e^{rT} \)
\( \lim_{N \to \infty} Nr_N = rT \)
where \( r_N \) is the rate of the riskless bond.

Assume further that
\( R^{(N)}_k = \frac{S^{(N)}_k - S^{(N)}_{k-1}}{S^{(N)}_{k-1}} \)
are i.i.d. and satisfy \( -1 < \alpha_N \leq R^{(N)}_k \leq \beta_N \)
s.t. \( \lim_{N \to \infty} \alpha_N = \lim_{N \to \infty} \beta_N = 0 \),
additionally \( \sigma_N^2 = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{k}^N \text{var}_N(R^{(N)}_k) \to \sigma^2 \).

Theorem 5.53. Under the above assumptions, the distributions of \( S^{(N)}_N \) under \( P^\ast_N \) converge weakly to the 
log-normal distribution with parameters \(\log S_0 + rT - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 T\) and \(\sigma \sqrt{T}\), i.e., to the distribution of
\[
S_T := S_0 \exp \left( \sigma W_T + \left( r - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \right) T \right),
\]
where \( W_T \) has a centered normal law \( N(0,T) \) with variance \( T \).

Example 5.55. Suppose the approximating model in the \( N \)th stage is a CRR model with interest rate
\[
r_N = \frac{r \, T}{N},
\]
and with returns \( R^{(N)}_k \), which can take the two possible values \( a_N \) and \( b_N \); see Section 5.5. We assume that
\[
\hat{a}_N = 1 + a_N = e^{-\sigma \sqrt{T/N}} \quad \text{and} \quad \hat{b}_N = 1 + b_N = e^{\sigma \sqrt{T/N}}
\]
for some given \(\sigma > 0\). Since
\[
\sqrt{N} r_N \to 0, \quad \sqrt{N} a_N \to -\sigma \sqrt{T}, \quad \sqrt{N} b_N \to \sigma \sqrt{T} \quad \text{as} \quad N \to \infty,
\]
we have \( a_N < r_N < b_N \) for large enough \( N \). 
Theorem 5.40 yields that the \( N \)th model is arbitrage-free and admits a unique equivalent martingale measure \( P^*_N \). 
The measure \( P^*_N \) is characterized by
\[
P^*_N \left[ R^{(N)}_k = b_N \right] =: p^*_N = \frac{r_N - a_N}{b_N - a_N}.
\]
and we obtain from (5.30) that
\[
    \lim_{N \to \infty} p^\ast_N = \frac{1}{2}
\]
Moreover \( E^\ast_N[R^{(N)}_k] = r_N \) and
\( \sum_{k=1}^N \text{var}_N(R^{(N)}_k) \to \sigma^2T \).

For a derivative \( C^{(N)} = f(S^{(N)}_N) \), one obtains then in the limit
for bounded and a.s. cont. \( f \), 
\( \lim_{N \to \infty} E^\ast_N [ \frac{C^{(N)}}{(1 + r_N)^N}] = \frac{e^{-rT}}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(S_0 e^{\sigma \sqrt{T}y + rT - \frac{\sigma^2 T}{2}}) e^{-\frac{y^2}{2}} \).

Explain the framework of state-space markets

For simplicity, asset \( 0 \) is supposed to be a savings account with \( S_0^0 = 1 \) and with risk-free one-period interest rate \( r > -1 \) 
such that \( S_1^0 = 1 + r \).

Notation:

Vector of initial prices: \( S_0 = (S_0^0, S_0^1, \ldots, S_0^d)^\top \)
(Random) Vector of terminal prices: \( S_1 = (S_1^0, S_1^1, \ldots, S_1^d)^\top \)
Payoff matrix:
\[
\boldsymbol{S}_1 = (S_1^i(\omega_k))_{1 \leq k \leq K, 0 \leq i \leq d} = 
\begin{pmatrix}
    S_1^0(\omega_1) & S_1^1(\omega_1) & \dots & S_1^d(\omega_1) \\
    \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    S_1^0(\omega_K) & S_1^1(\omega_K) & \dots & S_1^d(\omega_K)
\end{pmatrix}
\]

The tuple \( (S_0, \boldsymbol{S}_1) \) is called a market model.
A trading stategy is then given by \( \vartheta \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1} \),
the initial investment \( V_0^{\vartheta} = S^\top_0 \vartheta \) and terminal wealth \( V_1^{\vartheta} = \boldsymbol{S}_1 \vartheta \)
A claim 

Replicating trading strategy:

A contingent claim - defined as random variable \( C_1 : \Omega \to \mathbb{R} \) or in terms of the associated vector \( \boldsymbol{C}_1 \) of 
realizations, respectively - is replicable, if there exists a trading strategy \( \vartheta \) such that
  \[
  C_1 = V_1^{\vartheta} = \boldsymbol{S}_1^\top \vartheta \quad \text{or in vector notation} \quad \boldsymbol{C}_1 = \boldsymbol{V}_1^{\vartheta} = \boldsymbol{S}_1 \vartheta.
  \]
In this case, \( \vartheta \) is called a replicating trading strategy.
Note that \( \vartheta \) is a solution of the linear system of equations 
\(\boldsymbol{C}_1 = \boldsymbol{V}_1^{\vartheta} = \boldsymbol{S}_1 \vartheta. \)

Arbitrage

A trading strategy \( \vartheta \) is called arbitrage strategy if \( V_0^{\vartheta} \leq 0 \) and 
\( V_1^{\vartheta} = \boldsymbol{S}_1 \vartheta \geq 0 \) and there exists \( k \in \{1, \ldots, K\} \) such that \((V_1^{\vartheta})_k > 0. \)

If all contingent claims are replicable a market is complete. From linear algebra, \( \text{rk}(S_1) = K \) implies market completeness.


What are State-Price Vectors and how are they related to FTAP? 

\( \psi \in \mathbb{R}^{K}_{>0} \) with \( S_0 = \mathbf{S}_1^\top \psi \) is a state price vector.
It holds \( 1 = S^0_0 = (1+r)\sum_{k=1}^K \psi_k \) by construction.
Extend the market with \( K \) so called Arrow-Debreu securities, which satisfy \( \hat{S}_0^k = \psi_k \) and \( \hat{S}_1^k = e_k \).
Any replicable claim based on such securities, which maintains arbitrage freeness is characterized by
\( S_0^\top \vartheta = \sum_{k=1}^K (\mathbf{S}_1 \vartheta)_k \psi_k = \psi^\top \mathbf{S}_1 \vartheta = \psi^\top \mathbf{V}_1^\vartheta \)
implying \( S_0 = \mathbf{S}_1^\top \psi \), so they're state price vectors.
Note that this linear system of equations can also be used to determine \( \psi \) (although not necessarily uniquely).

Moreover there's a bijection between risk neutral probability measures \( \mathbb{Q} \) and state price vectors \( \psi \):
For such \( \mathbb{Q} \) we have
\[ S^\top_0 \vartheta = \frac{1}{1+r}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[V_1^\vartheta] = \frac{1}{1+r}(\mathbb{Q}[\omega_1], \dots, \mathbb{Q}[\omega_k])^\top \mathbf{V}_1^\vartheta \]
Conversely \( \psi \) defines \( \mathbb{Q} \) via \( \mathbb{Q}(\omega_k) = (1+r) \psi_k \), since
\( \sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbb{Q}[\omega_k] = (1 + r)\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbb{Q}[\psi_k] = \frac{1+r}{1+r} = 1 \).

Even if \( \psi \) is not uniquely determined, it gives the bounds for arbitrage free prices of a claim \( \mathbf{C}_1 \)
via \( (\text{inf}_{\psi} \psi^\top \mathbf{C}_1, \text{sup}_{\psi} \psi^\top \mathbf{C}_1) \)

Therefore FTAP is reformulated as 

1. Fundamental theorem of asset pricing:
For a state-space market \( (S_0, \boldsymbol{S}_1) \), the following statements are equivalent:
a) The market is arbitrage-free.
b) There exists a state-price vector \( \psi \).
c) There exists an equivalent risk-neutral measure \( \mathbb{Q} \).

2. Fundamental theorem of asset pricing:
Suppose that \( (S_0, \boldsymbol{S}_1) \) is an arbitrage-free state-space market. Then the following statements are equivalent:
a) The market is complete.
b) The state-price vector \( \psi \) is unique.
c) There exists exactly one equivalent risk-neutral measure \( \mathbb{Q} \).



Apply the State-Space Market model to determine arbitrage free prices

Consider the following state space market with two primary assets and three scenarios 
\(\Omega = \{\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3\}\), 
\(\mathcal{F} = \mathcal{P}(\Omega)\), 
\(\mathbb{P}[\{\omega_i\}] > 0\), \(i \in \{1, 2, 3\}\), defined by:

\[
\boldsymbol{S}_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol{S}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 
\frac{10}{9} & \frac{60}{9} \\
\frac{9}{9} & \frac{40}{9} \\
\frac{10}{9} & \frac{30}{9} 
\end{pmatrix}
\]

Questions:

1. Is the market model free of arbitrage? Determine all state-price vectors and the corresponding risk-neutral probability measures.
2. What can you say about market completeness?
3. Calculate the interval of arbitrage-free prices for the Contingent Claim \(C_1\), defined by

\(C_1(\omega_1) = 75, \quad C_1(\omega_2) = 50, \quad C_1(\omega_3) = 25.\)

Solving this linear system of equations yields 
\(\psi_2 = -3 \psi_1 + \frac{9}{5}, \quad \psi_3 = 2 \psi_1 - \frac{9}{10}.\)

Note that \(\psi_2 > 0, \psi_3 > 0\) if and only if \(\psi_1 \in \left( \frac{9}{20}, \frac{3}{5} \right)\). 
Thus, the set of state-price vectors is non-empty and given by

\[
\psi = \{\psi \in \mathbb{R}^3: \psi_1 \in \left( \frac{9}{20}, \frac{3}{5} \right), \psi_2 = -3 \psi_1 + \frac{9}{5}, \psi_3 = 2 \psi_1 - \frac{9}{10} \},
\]

i.e., the market model is arbitrage-free. Multiplying the state-price vector with 
\( (1 + r) = \frac{10}{9} \) yields the set of risk-neutral measures (\( q_i := \mathbb{Q}[\{\omega_i\}] \).

\[
\mathbb{Q} = \{\mathbb{Q}: q_1 = \frac{10}{9} \psi_1, q_2 = \frac{10}{9}(-3 \psi_1 + \frac{9}{5}), q_3 = \frac{10}{9}(2 \psi_1 - \frac{9}{10}), \psi_1 \in \left( \frac{9}{20}, \frac{3}{5} \right) \}
\]
\[
= \{\mathbb{Q}_\alpha: q_1 = \alpha, q_2 = -3\alpha + 2, q_3 = 2\alpha - 1, \alpha \in \left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{2}{3}\right) \}.
\]

An arbitrage-free state-space market is complete if and only if the state-price vector is unique. 
The results of 1. thus show that the given state-space market is incomplete.
The set of arbitrage-free prices is given by 
\(\left( \inf_{\psi \in \psi} \psi^\top \boldsymbol{C}_1, \sup_{\psi \in \psi} \psi^\top \boldsymbol{C}_1 \right).\)
Since 
\(\psi^\top \boldsymbol{C}_1 = 75 \psi_1 + 50 \cdot \left(-3 \psi_1 + \frac{9}{5} \right) + 25 \cdot \left(2 \psi_1 - \frac{9}{10} \right) = -25 \psi_1 + 67.5\)
and \(\psi_1 \in \left( \frac{9}{20}, \frac{3}{5} \right)\), we obtain the price interval \((52.5; 56.25).\)
